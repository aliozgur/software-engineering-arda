# Curriculum Audit

This repository expands the original plan (operating systems, relational/non-relational databases, Git,
networking, message brokers, web/mobile/desktop applications, and Python/JavaScript/C#/Go) into a
software-engineering apprenticeship.

## Important additions made during the audit

The original topic list was strong but technology-oriented. The following areas were added because a
well-rounded software engineer needs them and because they are commonly under-taught when learning
frameworks independently:

1. **Data structures, algorithms and complexity** - foundational problem solving.
2. **Computer architecture and C/memory** - prerequisite mental model for operating systems and performance.
3. **Requirements and domain modelling** - learning to define the problem before coding.
4. **Software design, architecture and refactoring** - modularity, coupling/cohesion, ADRs and changeability.
5. **Testing strategy and quality engineering** - unit, integration, system and risk-based testing.
6. **Concurrency and parallel/asynchronous reasoning** - races, locks, cancellation and deadlocks.
7. **HTTP/API design and compatibility** - protocols, contracts, errors, versioning and evolution.
8. **Security** - threat modelling, authentication/authorization, OWASP and dependency/supply-chain hygiene.
9. **Containers and CI/CD** - reproducibility, automation and release discipline.
10. **Observability and incident response** - logs, metrics, traces, runbooks and postmortems.
11. **Performance engineering** - profiling, load testing, capacity and evidence-based optimization.
12. **Distributed systems** - replication, consistency, consensus concepts, idempotency and partial failure.
13. **Release engineering and open-source practice** - SemVer, licensing, changelogs and maintainership.
14. **Professional engineering practice** - technical writing, design reviews, estimation and retrospectives.
15. **Responsible AI-assisted engineering** - disclosure, verification, task-specific AI policies and human accountability.

Desktop UI development is intentionally **not a core track**. The curriculum teaches web and mobile application
engineering and the underlying systems principles. A desktop framework can be learned later when a real project
requires it. Likewise, Kubernetes is not a core requirement: containers, networking, distributed-systems reasoning
and CI/CD come first.

The curriculum is designed to complement a mathematics/computer-science university degree. It deliberately avoids
trying to replace university mathematics, discrete mathematics, probability, graph theory, optimization or formal
CS courses when those are already being studied academically.
